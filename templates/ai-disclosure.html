AI Disclosure

Last updated: August 15, 2026 · v1.0

{{ phosphor('robot', 18, 'legal-inline-icon') }} You are talking to an AI. A language model writes the answers on Sh'elah, and nobody reads or checks an answer before it reaches you. This page explains how that works, where it can go wrong, and what the AI does not do.

1. Which AI models answer your questions

Sh'elah sends your question to Google Gemini first. If Gemini is not available, Anthropic's Claude answers instead. After Gemini fails twice in a row, Sh'elah stops asking it for a while and uses Claude alone, then tries Gemini again on a later question.

Provider Role
Google Gemini First choice for every answer
Anthropic Claude Answers when Gemini is not available

An answer does not say which of the two wrote it. Source passages that come without a translation can be translated by separate online services (MyMemory and Google Translate), not by these models.

Sh'elah does not use your questions or answers to train AI models without your explicit consent. What each provider receives is listed in the Privacy Policy, §4 and §9, and each provider's own terms cover what it does with that data.

2. Where the answers come from

Before the model writes anything, Sh'elah searches for sources and gives what it finds to the model along with your question. The technical name for this is retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. The search covers:

  • Sefaria, for the text of the Torah, Talmud, halachic codes and commentaries.
  • Halachipedia, a wiki of halachic sources and discussion.
  • Community customs, from a set of entries that Sh'elah keeps in its own database.
  • Hebcal, for dates, holidays and zmanim.
  • Wikipedia, only as a last resort when the Jewish sources have nothing on the question.

The model also sees the earlier messages of the same conversation. If you are signed in, it sees short summaries of your two most recent earlier questions as well. It does not see your name or email.

Starting from real texts keeps answers closer to the sources than a chatbot answering from memory. It does not make them correct. The search can bring back the wrong passage, or one that only touches the topic, and the model can still misread a good one.

3. Where it can go wrong

Language models sometimes state something confidently that is wrong, made up, or not in the sources they were given. On Sh'elah that usually looks like one of these:

  • A citation that looks real but points to the wrong place, or to a text that does not exist.
  • One opinion presented as the only one, when the sources disagree.
  • A passage that matches the topic but not the question.
  • Meaning that shifts when a Hebrew or Aramaic text is put into English, or the other way around.

Some things help. The model is told to cite only sources it is sure exist. A check on the finished answer catches explicit content and leaked instructions, though not factual mistakes. Citations link to the source text where Sh'elah can match it, so you can read the original yourself. These reduce the risk and do not remove it. Open the source before you rely on an answer (see Terms of Service §6).

4. What the AI does not do

  • It does not give a psak halacha. An answer is study material, not a ruling.
  • It does not replace your rabbi or posek.
  • It does not give legal, medical, mental-health or financial advice.
  • It is told never to claim to be a rabbi or a person.
  • It does not answer when a message sounds like an emergency, self-harm or abuse. Sh'elah shows a fixed message instead, pointing to emergency services, a crisis line or a trusted adult, and the model is never called. Everyday health questions with a halachic angle, such as fasting with a medical condition, are answered like any other question.
  • It declines hateful, violent or explicit requests, and subjects that have nothing to do with Jewish learning.

5. Your rabbi makes the call

Every answer comes with a note: AI-generated educational information, not a halachic ruling, ask your rabbi for practical guidance. The note stays on screen and cannot be turned off. Sh'elah is meant to help you find and understand sources faster. If you plan to act on an answer, your rabbi decides.

6. Age and sensitive topics

You must be at least 13 to use Sh'elah, and at least 16 in the EU and UK (see Terms of Service §3). The AI is told to assume the reader could be 13. On sensitive subjects such as niddah, mikveh and intimacy, it explains the halachic framework in clinical language, cites sources, and sends you to a rabbi, teacher or parent for practical details. If an answer comes back with explicit content anyway, a check replaces it with a short explanation and a referral.
